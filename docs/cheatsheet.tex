\textbf{Pitch (read aloud):}
``EdgeBot is a warehouse robot you control by voice. You say \emph{move the red box from shelf A to the
conveyor}. It understands the sentence, plans a route, and then a \textbf{reinforcement-learning policy trained with
PPO} drives it through clutter using a 24-beam lidar. It picks the box, delivers it and replies by voice. My own
contribution is a \textbf{lidar safety shield}: before any action reaches the motors, it predicts half a second
ahead and blocks moves that would crash. It needs no map and no retraining, and it brought collisions to
\textbf{zero}, even for 8-bit compressed models made for small edge chips.''}}

\begin{multicols}{2}

\hd{1. Workflow: what happens at each step}
Example command: \emph{``move the red box from shelf A to the conveyor''}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \lbl{Hear} \textbf{Whisper} (speech recognition) turns your voice into text.
\item \lbl{Understand} A small \textbf{BERT} model finds the \emph{intent} (pick and place) and the \emph{slots}
(colour = red, object = box, from = shelf A, to = conveyor).
\item \lbl{Check} The \textbf{dialogue manager} checks the request against what the robot knows. If something is
missing (``pick up the box''), it asks ``Which colour?'' and remembers your answer.
\item \lbl{Plan} \textbf{A*} search on the warehouse map gives a rough route as a list of waypoints.
\item \lbl{Drive (RL)} The \textbf{PPO policy} reads the lidar and the direction to the next waypoint, and outputs
speed and turning ten times a second. It steers around boxes the map didn't know about.
\item \lbl{Protect (novelty)} The \textbf{shield} checks every PPO action half a second ahead. If it would crash,
the shield swaps in the nearest safe move.
\item \lbl{Pick / place} The \textbf{arm} uses exact geometry (inverse kinematics) to grab and release the box.
\item \lbl{Reply} \textbf{Text-to-speech} says ``Picked up the red box\ldots{} delivered it to the conveyor.''
\end{enumerate}
\say{Learn what is hard to model, which is driving through unknown clutter, and calculate what is known
exactly: the route on the map and the arm geometry.}

\hd{2. The RL problem in words}
\begin{itemize}
\item \lbl{Agent} the robot. \lbl{Environment} the warehouse.
\item \lbl{State} what the robot senses: 24 lidar distances, how far and in which direction the goal is, and its last action.
\item \lbl{Action} how fast to go forward and how much to turn.
\item \lbl{Reward} points for moving closer to the goal, a big bonus for arriving, a big penalty for crashing, and small penalties for wasting time or driving jerkily.
\item \lbl{Goal of learning} find the behaviour (policy) that collects the most reward over time.
\end{itemize}

\hd{3. The algorithms: ``Q $\to$ D $\to$ P $\to$ S''}
Each one fixes the weakness of the one before.

\textbf{Q --- Q-learning (and SARSA)}\\
\lbl{What happens} The robot keeps a table with a score for every (place, move) pair, called a Q-value. After
each move it compares the score it expected with what it actually got plus the best it can expect next, and
nudges the table toward that. This difference is the \emph{temporal-difference (TD) error}. Over many trials the
table learns which move is best in every place. Q-learning learns the best route even while exploring
(\emph{off-policy}). SARSA learns from the moves it actually makes (\emph{on-policy}), so it prefers safer routes.\\
\lbl{Used in project} On a small grid version of the warehouse with an oil spill, to show the RL basics. Q-learning
found the shortest route, and SARSA found a safer one further from the spill.\\
\lbl{Source} Sutton \& Barto, \emph{Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction} (2018).\\
\lbl{Limit} A table can't list every possible lidar reading, because they are continuous numbers.

\textbf{D --- DQN (Deep Q-Network)}\\
\lbl{What happens} A neural network replaces the table: lidar in, a score for each move out. Three ideas keep it
stable. A \emph{replay buffer} stores past experiences and trains on random samples, so it doesn't overfit to the
last few seconds. A \emph{target network} is a slowly updated copy that gives steady learning targets. In
\emph{Double DQN}, one network picks the best next move and the other scores it, which stops it from being
over-optimistic.\\
\lbl{Used in project} The baseline driver with 5 fixed moves (forward, forward-left, forward-right, spin left, spin
right), from lidar and also from a camera-like image. 86\% success.\\
\lbl{Source} Mnih et al., \emph{Nature} (2015); van Hasselt et al., Double DQN, AAAI (2016).\\
\lbl{Limit} It can only choose from fixed moves, but real motors need smooth speeds.

\textbf{P --- PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) --- deployed}\\
\lbl{What happens} Two networks learn together. The \emph{actor} directly outputs the action (speed and turn). The
\emph{critic} predicts how good the current situation is. After a batch of driving, PPO asks: ``was this action
better or worse than the critic expected?'' (the \emph{advantage}). It makes good actions more likely and bad ones
less likely, but \emph{clips} each update so no action's probability changes by more than 20\% at once. That clip
is what keeps PPO stable.\\
\lbl{Used in project} It is the robot's real driver: 16 simulated robots train in parallel for 5 million steps
(about 25 minutes). 96\% success on 300 warehouses it never saw. The final model is only 26 KB in 8-bit.\\
\lbl{Why I chose it} It gives continuous, smooth control, more success than DQN (96\% vs 86\%), stable training, and
it is the standard algorithm in recent robot-navigation research.\\
\lbl{Source} Schulman et al., PPO (2017), with advantage estimation (GAE) from Schulman et al., ICLR (2016).\\
\lbl{Limit} It still crashes 2--3\% of the time, and slightly more after 8-bit compression.

\textbf{S --- Lidar safety shield (my novelty)}\\
\lbl{What happens} At every step: PPO proposes a move, and the shield turns the lidar scan into obstacle points. It
imagines the robot holding that move for the next half second, using the robot's own motion equations. If the
imagined path comes too close to any obstacle, it replaces the move with the closest safe one: slower, a turn, or
a spin on the spot.\\
\lbl{Why it is new} It needs no map, no training and no extra neural network, and it works on any policy, including
8-bit compressed ones.\\
\lbl{Result} Collisions dropped to \textbf{0\%} for all 5 policies tested. It only steps in on about 5\% of moves, and
success stayed about the same (PPO 8-bit even improved from 95.7\% to 97.0\%).
